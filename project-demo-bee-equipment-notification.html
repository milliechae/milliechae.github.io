A real-time equipment monitoring dashboard for manufacturing that turns scattered alerts into a single, prioritized workflow.

Working closely with PMs, engineers, and data analysts, I turned complex real-time equipment data into an interface that surfaces only the issues that need a human's attention.

PAIN POINT

Operators spent more time finding the right information than acting on it.

Operators had access to all the data they needed, but not the clarity to understand what required attention first. Critical alerts were often buried among routine notifications, making quick decision-making increasingly difficult.

  • Hundreds of real-time alerts
    Large volumes of notifications made it difficult to identify what truly required immediate attention.
  • Difficult prioritization
    Critical equipment issues were easily mixed with routine operational updates.
  • Frequent context switching
    Operators constantly moved between multiple screens before understanding the full situation.
✦ DESIGN CHALLENGE

How might we help operators spot the alerts that matter most, at a glance?

Show only what's relevant, so operators can cut through the noise and act faster.

USER RESEARCH

Understanding how operators actually work.

Before exploring interface solutions, I focused on understanding how operators monitored equipment, responded to anomalies, and searched for information throughout their daily workflow. Through interviews, workflow analysis, and collaborative discussions with domain experts, several behavioral patterns consistently emerged.

01

Operators searched by task, not hierarchy.

Rather than navigating complex equipment trees, operators relied on search, recently viewed equipment, and production context to locate information quickly.

02

Not every alert deserved equal attention.

Users mentally separated notifications into critical, monitor, and ignore. Existing interfaces treated every alert equally, increasing cognitive load.

03

Context switching slowed response time.

Investigating a single anomaly often required opening multiple pages before enough context was available to make a decision.

04

Many alerts weren't real faults at all.

Missing or spiking sensor values triggered alerts just like real anomalies. Operators checked each one by hand, and many turned out to mean nothing. A large share of their effort went into alerts that never needed a human.

✦ DESIGN HYPOTHESIS

Four hypotheses guided the redesign of the monitoring experience.

I translated the research findings into four testable hypotheses that guided my design decisions before moving into wireframes and prototypes.

01  Prioritize critical information

If critical alerts are surfaced before secondary information, operators will recognize urgent issues faster.

02  Reduce context switching

If monitoring and issue reporting happen in one continuous workflow, unnecessary navigation will decrease.

03  Make information instantly scannable

If equipment status can be understood at a glance, cognitive load during monitoring will be reduced.

04  Stop alerts that don't matter

If bad sensor data is filtered out before it becomes an alert, operators will spend less time on checks that never needed them.

Operators don't know the right number in advance. They tune by watching how their data reacts. Numeric input hid that reaction until after applying, and presets took away per-machine control. The slider was the only option that kept both: immediate feedback and fine control.

Design Solution

Designing around workflows, not just data.

Instead of displaying every data point equally, I designed the dashboard around operator workflows: prioritizing critical information, cutting unnecessary navigation, and helping operators move from detection to action as quickly as possible.

Lists, not category trees.

Usability tests showed that operators didn't want to dig through a category tree to find equipment. So I replaced it with a simple list where they can pin priority items to the top or bottom and use contextual search to find what they need right away.

Prioritized, color-balanced cards.

I worked with data analysts to define which metrics mattered most and set a clear information hierarchy for each card. Status, MTBF, and uptime sit together in every card, and a balanced color system keeps the screen readable without feeling monotonous.

Record an issue the moment it happens.

I designed an anomaly input popup so operators can log an issue the moment it happens, without leaving the dashboard. Auto-suggestions pull in existing issue records or let them add new related data on the spot.

Impact

Faster decisions, less digging.

By designing around operator workflows, I removed unnecessary steps between seeing an alert and acting on it. Critical data became easier to reach, and real-time alerts fit into one continuous flow, easing the daily cognitive load of triaging alerts.

Reflection

Removing steps mattered more than adding features.

Real-time monitoring tools succeed or fail by what they leave out. The fewer steps operators had between seeing an alert and acting on it, the faster they could decide. Next time, I'd set baseline metrics before launch so the impact could be measured, not just heard.
